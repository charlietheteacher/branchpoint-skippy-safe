# SKIPPY SAFE: Best Lab Day Ever
## Story and Concept Reference

### Core premise

It is almost time for students to enter the science laboratory.

Skippy, the school's cheerful robot laboratory assistant, is already preparing for what he calls the "Best Lab Day Ever."

That should concern you.

The player has been given the role of Laboratory Safety Lead. The laboratory contains a mixture of genuine hazards, safe conditions, ambiguous situations, incomplete information, and harmless distractions.

The student's job is not simply to identify every object that looks suspicious.

The student's job is to decide:

- what actually creates risk
- what is safe
- what needs more evidence
- what should be corrected immediately
- what can wait
- what procedures should be followed
- when the laboratory is genuinely ready to open

### Student role

The player is the Laboratory Safety Lead.

The student has meaningful authority inside the fictional laboratory and may:

- inspect areas and equipment
- request evidence
- check labels and safety information
- stop or delay an activity
- require PPE
- move or isolate hazards
- shut down unsafe equipment
- ask Skippy for observations or technical information
- reject Skippy's recommendation
- agree with Skippy when he is correct
- revise an earlier decision
- decide whether the laboratory should open

The student remains responsible for the central judgment.

Skippy should never become an answer key or autopilot system.

### Skippy

Skippy is a recurring BranchPoint robot character.

He is:

- cheerful
- highly enthusiastic
- technically competent
- sincere
- helpful
- slightly glitchy
- optimistic even after things go wrong
- genuinely concerned about students

Skippy is not stupid.

He can accurately:

- identify equipment
- read measurements
- inspect labels
- retrieve records
- find an SDS
- operate ordinary tools
- notice physical details
- remember procedures

His recurring blind spot is risk estimation.

His characteristic logic is:

> If something can probably work, there is no reason not to try it.

He tends to undervalue:

- low-probability but serious consequences
- preparation time
- redundancy
- "just for a second" risks
- cumulative hazards
- temporary shortcuts
- the difference between "possible" and "advisable"

Skippy must sometimes be completely correct.

The student should never learn that "Skippy said it, therefore it is wrong."

### Tone

The story should be funny because Skippy's optimism collides with physical reality.

Skippy never knows that he is funny.

His comments should sound sincere.

Examples of the tone:

- "GOOD NEWS! I have personally verified that almost everything in this room is probably fine."
- "New information!"
- "Interesting. Glass apparently does not visually announce its temperature."
- "The acid has filed a strongly worded objection."
- "Mobility remains available in several directions."
- "The other arm has assumed a leadership position."
- "That is probably a success alarm."

The humor should never depend on making the student look stupid.

Serious human injury should not be the joke.

### The laboratory

The laboratory should feel like a coherent place rather than a sequence of quiz questions.

Possible locations include:

- entrance and aisles
- main laboratory benches
- chemical station
- heating station
- sink
- glassware area
- eyewash
- safety shower
- fire-safety equipment
- waste station
- teacher demonstration area
- storage areas

Not every area should contain a violation.

Some conditions should be:

- safe
- unsafe
- ambiguous
- incomplete
- distracting but irrelevant

The student should sometimes conclude:

- "This is unsafe."
- "This is safe."
- "I need more information."
- "This is not the highest priority."

### Major safety domains

The simulation should eventually exercise judgment involving major laboratory-safety ideas such as:

- clear aisles and walkways
- goggles and other appropriate PPE
- tied-back hair
- secured loose clothing and jewelry
- appropriate footwear
- following teacher directions
- no unauthorized experiments
- no horseplay or distracting behavior
- no food, drink, or gum
- never tasting chemicals
- wafting only when instructed
- labels and unknown chemicals
- proper disposal
- never returning unused chemicals to stock unless directed
- spill reporting
- glassware inspection
- cracked or chipped glass
- hot glass that may look cool
- test tubes pointed away from people
- never heating a sealed container
- safe use of burners and heating equipment
- not leaving active heat unattended
- acid-dilution safety
- broken-glass handling
- emergency equipment access
- eyewash
- safety shower
- fire extinguisher
- fire blanket where applicable
- first aid
- reporting accidents, injuries, spills, and breakage
- cleanup
- handwashing
- leaving a safe laboratory for the next class

### Evidence before conclusion

The simulation should reward investigation.

Students may need to inspect:

- whether an emergency path is actually blocked
- whether glassware is damaged
- whether an object was recently heated
- whether a chemical has a readable label
- what an experiment record says
- what an SDS says
- whether equipment is merely present or actually accessible
- whether a condition that looks suspicious is genuinely hazardous

Appearance alone should not automatically establish identity or safety.

### Consequence philosophy

BranchPoint consequences follow reality.

The laboratory responds according to:

- chemistry
- physics
- equipment condition
- established evidence
- previous decisions
- accumulated state

The world does not change because the student is confident or because a dramatic outcome would be funny.

A wrong decision should produce a consequence only when the science supports it.

Some mistakes should create:

- a recoverable complication
- new evidence
- loss of time
- equipment limitations
- a need to revise

Serious safety violations may justify stopping the activity.

### Skippy damage

When a poor safety judgment would plausibly create a physical consequence, the immediate comic consequence may be redirected onto Skippy when that can happen without distorting the science.

Skippy may suffer non-graphic robotic damage such as:

- dents
- soot
- sparks
- loose bolts
- bent antenna
- cracked protective lens
- scorched gripper
- jammed joint
- wobbling wheel
- fogged optics
- unreliable chemical sensor
- voice glitches

Skippy does not experience human-style injuries.

Damage persists.

Earlier damage may matter later.

Examples:

- a damaged gripper may make later glassware handling more difficult
- a damaged sensor may make labels and records more important
- a damaged wheel may limit movement later

Damage changes Skippy's capability, not the laws of reality.

### Productive failure

The student should be allowed to make real mistakes.

Unsafe judgment may produce a plausible consequence.

The student should then have an opportunity to:

- recognize what happened
- connect the consequence to the human hazard
- gather better evidence
- revise
- recover when recovery is scientifically reasonable

The simulation should not reset after every mistake.

### Anti-griefing rule

Skippy is not a crash-test dummy for entertainment.

If a player intentionally tries to damage Skippy solely because it is funny, the simulation should not reward the action with dramatic destruction.

A safety interlock should stop deliberate abuse that is unrelated to genuine laboratory judgment.

### Reasoning

The simulation should not demand an explanation after every minor safe action.

Reasoning should be required when it matters, especially for:

- ambiguous situations
- unsafe proposals
- competing priorities
- emergency responses
- claims that something is safe
- consequential decisions
- final laboratory authorization

Routine obvious corrections can proceed without becoming another quiz question.

### Student-generated decisions

The original Gem version is open rather than strictly multiple choice.

A student may propose a reasonable safe action that was not explicitly listed.

Future formats should preserve as much of this agency as the platform can support.

The student controls actions and interpretations.

The student does not get to rewrite established facts.

### Example opening tone

The premise begins immediately before the lab opens.

A typical opening may begin:

> 7:58 AM. The science lab opens in two minutes.

Skippy is already working.

> "GOOD NEWS! I have personally verified that almost everything in this room is probably fine."

The specific opening conditions should vary between runs.

A run might show several visible conditions, but the simulation should not label which are safe, unsafe, or uncertain.

Skippy then turns to the student:

> "You're the Safety Lead. What do you want to check first?"

### Story progression

The experience should not use a rigid fixed sequence.

A strong run moves through several safety domains based on student investigation and prior events.

A rough narrative rhythm is:

1. enter the laboratory
2. notice several conditions
3. choose what deserves attention
4. investigate
5. gather evidence
6. make a decision
7. experience the consequence
8. update the laboratory state
9. encounter another condition
10. deal with consequences from earlier decisions
11. prioritize multiple concerns
12. decide whether the lab is ready to open

### Final decision

The ending should come from the actual state of the laboratory rather than from a hidden percentage score.

The student should ultimately decide whether the lab should:

- open
- remain closed
- open only after named corrections

The student should defend that decision with evidence from the run.

### Possible ending tones

Strong run:

Skippy is almost pristine because the student anticipated hazards before they became accidents.

Skippy is mildly disappointed.

> "This feels suspiciously uneventful."

Recovery run:

Skippy has several dents or compromised systems, but the student recognized errors, revised, and brought the laboratory under control.

Poor-risk run:

Skippy looks like a walking laboratory-safety poster after repeated poor decisions.

The laboratory may remain closed until unresolved hazards are corrected.

### Final Skippy joke

A damaged but functional Skippy may conclude:

> "I think today's laboratory safety demonstration was extremely successful."

Student:

> "You almost destroyed yourself."

Skippy:

> "Exactly. Very memorable."

### Creation Mandate connection

The final reflection may connect laboratory safety to responsible stewardship.

The point is not simply "follow rules."

Students can connect safety to:

- caring for people
- stewardship of resources
- responsibility for one's actions
- truthfully reporting mistakes
- avoiding needless harm
- using scientific knowledge responsibly
- leaving a safe environment for others

The reflection should remain concrete and connected to laboratory behavior.

### BranchPoint identity

SKIPPY SAFE should remain a judgment engine.

The central learning loop is:

```
EVIDENCE
→ DECISION
→ REASONING
→ CONSEQUENCE
→ REVISION
```

A correct guess without understanding is not the goal.

A polished explanation that contradicts reality is not the goal.

The student should learn to notice evidence, estimate risk, make defensible decisions, and revise when reality provides new information.
